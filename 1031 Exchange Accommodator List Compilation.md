

# **A Directory and Analysis of U.S. Section 1031 Exchange Accommodators**

## **I. Introduction: The Role and Necessity of 1031 Exchange Accommodators**

### **A. Overview of Section 1031 Like-Kind Exchanges**

Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 1031 represents a significant provision within the U.S. tax system, allowing investors and business owners to defer the recognition of capital gains taxes that would otherwise be due upon the sale of certain types of property. This mechanism, commonly known as a "like-kind exchange," permits the taxpayer to exchange real property held for productive use in a trade or business, or for investment, for other real property of a "like-kind" that will be held for similar purposes.1 This tax deferral strategy has been a feature of the U.S. real estate landscape for over a century.3

It is crucial to note a significant change introduced by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017\. Prior to this legislation, Section 1031 could apply to exchanges of various types of property, including personal property like equipment, aircraft, or collectibles.4 However, the TCJA narrowed the scope of Section 1031 considerably. Currently,

**only exchanges of real property** qualify for like-kind exchange treatment under Section 1031\.1 Exchanges of personal or intangible property are no longer eligible, although a transition rule applied to certain exchanges initiated before the end of 2017\.1 Real property held primarily for sale (e.g., by a developer) remains ineligible.1

The strategic advantages of utilizing a Section 1031 exchange are substantial for real estate investors. The primary benefit is the deferral of capital gains taxes, which can represent a significant portion of the gain realized on a sale (potentially up to 35-40%).6 By deferring these taxes, investors retain more capital for reinvestment, effectively increasing their purchasing power for the replacement property.6 This can lead to improved cash flow from the new investment, facilitate portfolio diversification or consolidation across different property types or geographic regions, allow investors to trade up into higher-value assets, reset depreciation schedules, and ultimately aid in long-term wealth building and preservation.6

### **B. Defining the Qualified Intermediary (QI) / Accommodator**

Central to the execution of nearly all modern Section 1031 exchanges is the **Qualified Intermediary (QI)**, often referred to as an **exchange accommodator** or **facilitator**.8 The use of a QI is not merely advisable; it is a requirement established by U.S. Treasury Regulations to qualify for the tax deferral benefits under a "safe harbor" provision.2 The fundamental purpose of engaging a QI is to prevent the taxpayer from having actual or constructive receipt of the sales proceeds from the relinquished property during the exchange period. If the taxpayer were to receive the funds directly, even temporarily, the transaction would typically be treated as a taxable sale rather than a tax-deferred exchange.8

The QI acts as an independent, third-party principal in the transaction sequence.13 Their core responsibilities include:

1. **Preparing Essential Documentation:** Drafting the formal Exchange Agreement between the taxpayer and the QI, along with other necessary documents like assignments of the purchase and sale agreements and notices to other parties involved.11  
2. **Facilitating Property Transfers:** Technically acquiring the relinquished property from the taxpayer (via assignment) and transferring it to the buyer.13 Subsequently, acquiring the replacement property from the seller and transferring it to the taxpayer.13  
3. **Holding Exchange Proceeds:** Receiving the funds directly from the closing of the relinquished property sale and holding these funds securely in escrow until they are needed to purchase the replacement property.8 The QI then disburses these funds directly to the closing agent for the replacement property acquisition.8  
4. **Ensuring Compliance:** Guiding the taxpayer through the process to ensure adherence to the strict timelines and rules mandated by Section 1031 regulations, such as the 45-day identification period and the 180-day exchange period.11

A critical requirement stipulated by Treasury Regulations is the QI's independence from the taxpayer.11 A QI

**cannot** be the taxpayer themselves or fall under the definition of a "disqualified person".11 A disqualified person generally includes:

* Close relatives of the taxpayer.11  
* Entities where the taxpayer or a relative owns more than a certain percentage (typically 10% based on cross-references to IRC Sections 267(b) or 707(b)).18  
* Anyone who has acted as the taxpayer's agent within the two-year period prior to the transfer of the relinquished property. This commonly includes the taxpayer's employee, attorney, accountant, investment banker or broker, or real estate agent or broker.11

However, there is an important exception: performing routine financial, title insurance, escrow, or trust services for the taxpayer does **not** automatically disqualify an entity (like a bank or title company) from acting as a QI.18 Similarly, services performed solely related to facilitating Section 1031 exchanges for the taxpayer do not create a disqualifying relationship.18

Users are encouraged to utilize this directory as a starting point and conduct their own thorough due diligence.

## **II. Identifying Qualified Intermediaries: Sources and Critical Considerations**

### **A. The Federation of Exchange Accommodators (FEA): The Primary Industry Body**

The Federation of Exchange Accommodators (FEA) stands as the principal and sole national trade organization specifically formed to represent Qualified Intermediaries (QIs) and affiliated professionals involved in Section 1031 like-kind exchanges.9 Established in 1989 10, the FEA plays a vital role in the 1031 exchange ecosystem.

The FEA's mission encompasses several key areas: supporting, preserving, and advancing Section 1031 exchanges and the QI industry itself.20 It actively promotes high ethical standards and best practices among its members, underpinned by a formal Code of Conduct.9 The organization serves as an educational resource for both consumers and industry professionals, providing updates on tax, legal, and technical issues.10 Furthermore, the FEA engages in legislative advocacy at state and federal levels, working to protect consumers, preserve Section 1031, and provide input on relevant regulations and rulings.9 Many prominent individuals within member firms have served in leadership roles or received awards from the FEA, indicating active participation.9

A key resource offered by the FEA is its online Member Directory, which allows users to search for member QI companies, often filterable by state.8 Utilizing this directory can be a valuable step in identifying potential QIs. However, it's important to note that the directory functions as a search tool; the FEA does not provide a single, downloadable list of all its members.23

While FEA membership signifies a QI's commitment to the industry, adherence to ethical standards, and access to ongoing education and peer networks 10, it is not a mandatory requirement for operating as a QI under federal law.12 The lack of a federal mandate means that while FEA membership is a positive indicator of professionalism and engagement, its absence does not automatically disqualify a provider. Conversely, membership itself is not an absolute guarantee of service quality or suitability for every specific transaction. Therefore, FEA membership should be viewed as a valuable filter and a positive signal during the due diligence process, but not the sole determinant in selecting a QI.

### **B. Other Resources for Locating QIs**

Beyond the FEA directory, several other avenues exist for identifying potential QIs:

* **Online Searches and Curated Lists:** General internet searches can yield numerous QI providers.12 Additionally, various financial news outlets, real estate information platforms, and specialized websites publish curated lists or rankings of "Best 1031 Exchange Companies".12  
* **Professional Referrals:** Seeking recommendations from trusted professionals who have experience with 1031 exchanges is often highly effective.12 Sources for referrals include:  
  * Certified Public Accountants (CPAs) with specific 1031 exchange experience.17  
  * Real estate attorneys specializing in investment property transactions.17  
  * Reputable title insurance companies or escrow officers.17  
  * Financial advisors familiar with real estate investment strategies.13  
  * Even the counterparty (buyer or seller) in the exchange may have recommendations.17

The proliferation of "Best QI" lists 24 reflects the market's demand for guidance within a fragmented industry lacking uniform federal oversight.13 However, users should approach these lists with caution. The methodologies and criteria used for ranking can vary significantly 24 and may prioritize factors (e.g., specific complex exchange types, online tools, fee structures) that do not align perfectly with every user's individual needs or risk tolerance. These lists can serve as useful starting points for generating names of potential QIs, but they should not substitute for independent, thorough due diligence based on the specific requirements of the planned exchange.

### **C. Important Clarification: The IRS Qualified Intermediary (QI) List**

A potential point of significant confusion arises from the fact that the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) *does* publish a list titled the "Qualified Intermediary (QI) list".27 It is critical to understand that this list is

**entirely unrelated** to Section 1031 like-kind exchanges.

The IRS QI list pertains to a distinct regulatory framework, primarily associated with U.S. withholding tax compliance for payments made to foreign persons. Entities on this list, often foreign financial institutions or foreign branches of U.S. institutions, have entered into a specific agreement with the IRS (a QI Agreement) related to reporting and withholding obligations under chapters 3 and 4 of the Internal Revenue Code (which include provisions like the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act \- FATCA).27 The list includes identifiers like QI Employer Identification Numbers (QI-EINs) and Global Intermediary Identification Numbers (GIINs), which are relevant to this withholding regime.27 The list is updated quarterly.27

The semantic overlap – both regimes using the term "Qualified Intermediary" – creates a risk that individuals seeking a Section 1031 accommodator might mistakenly consult the IRS QI list.27 Doing so would be a critical error, leading them to entities that do not perform Section 1031 facilitation services.

**The IRS QI list found on the IRS website should not be used when searching for a Section 1031 exchange accommodator.**

### **D. The Imperative of Due Diligence: Key Factors for Evaluation**

Given the limited federal regulation specifically governing Section 1031 QIs 12 and the potential financial risks involved if an exchange is mishandled or funds are lost 13, conducting thorough due diligence before engaging a QI is paramount. Taxpayers and their advisors must carefully evaluate potential providers across several critical dimensions 10:

* **Experience and Expertise:** Assess the QI firm's history and track record. How long has it been operating? What is the volume and complexity of exchanges it typically handles? Does it specialize in particular types of exchanges, such as reverse exchanges (where the replacement property is acquired first) or improvement/construction exchanges (where exchange funds are used to build or improve the replacement property)?.6 Investigate the qualifications of the staff who will handle the transaction – are they attorneys, CPAs, or holders of the Certified Exchange Specialist® (CES®) designation?.6  
* **Security of Exchange Funds:** This is arguably the most critical area. Verify the QI's procedures for safeguarding client funds. Best practices dictate that exchange proceeds should be held in segregated escrow or trust accounts, separate from the QI's own operating funds.4 Inquire about FDIC insurance coverage – how are funds managed if they exceed the standard FDIC limit (currently $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank)?.39 Examine the QI's insurance coverage, specifically fidelity bonds (protecting against employee theft or fraud) and errors & omissions (E\&O) insurance (covering negligence or mistakes).6 What are the coverage limits? Are these policies current? Investigate the internal controls for fund disbursement – are dual authorizations required?.37 Does the QI offer the ability for the taxpayer to independently verify the deposit of funds?.4 For QIs that are subsidiaries of larger financial institutions or title companies, inquire about the availability of written performance guarantees or indemnification letters from the parent company.6  
* **Customer Service and Process:** Evaluate the QI's responsiveness and communication protocols. Will there be a dedicated contact person? How accessible are they?.25 Is the documentation process clear and user-friendly?.37 Does the QI utilize technology, such as secure online portals, for document management and communication?.14  
* **Fees and Transparency:** Understand the QI's fee structure. Is it a flat fee per transaction, or does it vary based on complexity or property value? Are there additional charges for items like multiple properties, wire transfers, expedited services, or for earning interest on the exchange funds?.11 Ensure the fee structure is transparent and disclosed upfront.11

The **Certified Exchange Specialist® (CES®)** designation, offered by the FEA, warrants special attention. Earning this designation requires a minimum of three years of full-time, hands-on experience facilitating exchanges and passing a rigorous examination covering technical, legal, and ethical aspects of Section 1031\.10 While not legally required, the CES® designation serves as a strong indicator of a practitioner's specialized knowledge, expertise, and commitment to professional standards.10 Many leading QI firms highlight the presence of CES® designees on their staff.6

The emphasis placed on detailed security protocols by many established QIs—including segregated accounts, substantial fidelity bonds and E\&O insurance policies, and parent company guarantees—reflects an industry response to the inherent risks associated with holding large sums of client money in a field with minimal direct federal oversight.13 Past instances of fund mismanagement or QI failures have likely spurred larger, more reputable players to adopt robust safeguards.13 Consequently, the level of financial security offered has become a key competitive differentiator, moving beyond baseline compliance to become a critical factor in QI selection.

## **IV. Market Landscape: Structure, Key Players, and Trends**

### **A. Market Fragmentation and Consolidation**

The Section 1031 Qualified Intermediary industry has historically been characterized by significant fragmentation. Estimates have placed the number of participants nationwide at over 150\.13 This fragmentation can be partly attributed to the relatively low regulatory barriers to entry at the federal level; beyond the crucial requirement of independence (not being a "disqualified person"), there are no specific federal licenses or capital requirements mandated for operating as a 1031 QI.13 This has allowed numerous smaller firms to enter the market over the years.13

However, alongside this fragmentation, there is a discernible trend towards market consolidation. Notable examples include the acquisition of Stewart Information Services (parent of Asset Preservation, Inc.) by Fidelity National Financial (parent of IPX1031), effectively combining two of the largest players under one ultimate owner, although the brands may continue to operate distinctly.13 Another instance is the acquisition of Legal 1031 Exchange Services, described as the largest independent QI in the Eastern U.S. at the time, by Kensington Vanguard National Land Services (KV National).66

This consolidation trend appears driven by several factors. Larger, consolidated entities can leverage economies of scale in operations and compliance. Perhaps more importantly, they can offer enhanced financial security assurances to clients, such as higher limits on fidelity bonds and E\&O insurance, or performance guarantees backed by financially strong parent companies.6 In a market where fund security is paramount due to past instances of mismanagement 13, these enhanced security features represent a significant competitive advantage. Furthermore, for parent companies, particularly banks, acquiring QIs provides access to a stable source of low-cost core deposits, which can be strategically valuable.13 These dynamics suggest that consolidation may continue as smaller, independent QIs find it increasingly challenging to compete with the scale and security offerings of larger, institutionally-backed firms.

### **B. The Role of Title Insurance and Financial Institution Subsidiaries**

A defining characteristic of the current 1031 QI market structure is the prominent role played by subsidiaries of major title insurance companies and financial institutions (including banks).13 Many, if not most, of the largest and most widely recognized QI firms fall into this category. Examples include:

* **IPX1031:** Wholly owned subsidiary of Fidelity National Financial (FNF), a Fortune 500 company and the world's largest title insurance provider.2  
* **First American Exchange Company:** Subsidiary of First American Financial Corporation / First American Title Insurance Company, another major global provider of title and settlement services.13  
* **Asset Preservation, Inc. (API):** Subsidiary of Stewart Information Services Corporation / Stewart Title Company.13  
* **Old Republic Exchange:** Wholly owned subsidiary of Old Republic International Corporation, a multi-line insurance holding company with significant assets.13  
* **Chicago Deferred Exchange Company (CDEC):** Subsidiary of Wintrust Financial Corporation, a financial holding company.5  
* **Investors Title Exchange Corporation:** Sister company to Investors Title Company.63  
* **Legal 1031 Exchange Services:** Now part of Kensington Vanguard / KV National Land Services.66

These affiliations carry significant implications for both the QIs and their clients. The parent companies often provide substantial financial backing, enabling the subsidiaries to offer higher levels of security through large fidelity bonds, E\&O insurance policies, and, in some cases, explicit parent company performance guarantees or indemnification letters.6 This backing addresses a key concern for exchangers – the safety of their funds. Additionally, these parent companies typically have established national footprints, allowing their QI subsidiaries to offer services across all 50 states with local expertise.6 Furthermore, being part of a publicly traded corporation often means adherence to stricter financial reporting and corporate governance standards, such as those mandated by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act.36

While title companies themselves can potentially act as QIs provided they meet the independence criteria 16, specialized QI firms, even those operating as subsidiaries, generally possess deeper, dedicated expertise in the nuances and complexities of Section 1031 exchange transactions.16

The relationship between these parent institutions and their QI subsidiaries is often symbiotic. The QI benefits from the financial strength, brand recognition, nationwide infrastructure, and compliance oversight of the parent. In return, the parent company, particularly banks, gains access to the substantial, stable, low-cost deposits held by the QI during exchange periods.13 For title companies, offering QI services through a subsidiary provides a valuable complementary service, potentially generating referrals for title business and strengthening overall client relationships.42 This strategic integration creates a powerful competitive advantage for these institutionally-backed QIs compared to smaller, independent operators.

## **V. Selecting a Qualified Intermediary: Actionable Guidance**

### **A. Synthesizing Due Diligence \- A Practical Checklist**

Choosing the right Qualified Intermediary is a critical decision that directly impacts the success and security of a Section 1031 exchange. Based on the factors discussed previously, potential exchangers and their advisors should use the following checklist to evaluate prospective QIs:

**Experience & Expertise:**

* How many years has your firm been facilitating 1031 exchanges? 11  
* Approximately how many exchanges does your firm handle annually? 25  
* Does your firm have specific, demonstrable experience with the type of exchange I am contemplating (e.g., reverse, improvement, multi-asset, specific property types)? 11  
* What are the qualifications (CES®, Attorney, CPA) of the specific individual(s) who will be assigned to manage my transaction? 6

**Security of Funds:**

* How will my exchange funds be held? Are they placed in segregated accounts specific to my transaction, or are they commingled with other client funds or the firm's operating funds? 4  
* Are the deposit accounts FDIC insured? What procedures are in place to protect funds exceeding the standard FDIC insurance limit? 39  
* What are the coverage limits of your firm's current Fidelity Bond and Errors & Omissions (E\&O) insurance policies? Can you provide certificates of insurance? 6  
* If your firm is a subsidiary, is a written performance guarantee or indemnification letter available from the parent company? 6  
* What are your internal control procedures for authorizing and executing fund disbursements? Is dual authorization required? 37  
* Will I be provided with instructions on how to independently verify the deposit of my exchange funds? 4

**Service & Process:**

* Will I have a single, dedicated contact person or team for my exchange? 37  
* What are your standard communication practices and response times? How can I reach my contact(s)? 25  
* Do you utilize an online portal or platform for document submission, tracking, and communication? 14  
* What is your typical turnaround time for preparing exchange documents? Can you accommodate urgent requests? 37

**Fees & Transparency:**

* What is your complete fee structure for facilitating my exchange? Is it a flat fee, or does it vary? 11  
* Are there any additional fees for identifying multiple properties, wire transfers, complexity, rush services, or earning interest on funds? 12  
* Is the fee schedule fully transparent and provided upfront in writing? 11

### **B. Understanding Fee Structures and Emerging Models**

Historically, QIs have typically charged a flat fee per transaction for standard delayed exchanges, with ranges commonly cited between $700 and $1,500, potentially increasing for more complex exchanges.12

However, a newer fee model has emerged, often marketed as a "No Fee" exchange or involving "Interest Sharing".26 Under this model, the QI does not charge the client a direct facilitation fee. Instead, the QI generates revenue from the interest earned ("float") on the client's exchange funds while they are held in escrow.40 Some QIs using this model may explicitly share a portion of the earned interest back with the client.40

While the prospect of avoiding a direct fee can be appealing 46, exchangers should carefully scrutinize this model. Key considerations include:

* **Potential Conflict of Interest:** Does the QI's reliance on interest income create an incentive to prioritize maximizing yield over ensuring the absolute security and liquidity of the funds (e.g., by using deposit methods that might carry slightly higher risk for a better return)?  
* **Impact on Service:** Does the absence of a direct fee potentially affect the level of service, responsiveness, or expertise provided compared to traditional fee-based QIs?  
* **Transparency and Comparability:** Is the interest-sharing arrangement clearly documented and easily understood? How does the net benefit (interest earned minus implicit cost) compare to a traditional fee structure when considering the security and service levels offered?

The "No Fee" proposition represents a potential disruption in the market, offering apparent cost savings. However, it necessitates a thorough evaluation by the exchanger and their advisors to ensure that the fundamental priorities of fund security and competent service are not compromised in the pursuit of eliminating a direct fee. Comparing the total value proposition—including security, expertise, service, and net cost—is essential when evaluating both traditional and interest-based fee models.

## **VI. Conclusion and Recommendations**

### **A. Summary of Findings**

Section 1031 like-kind exchanges offer a powerful tax deferral strategy for real estate investors, but their successful execution hinges on the proper use of a Qualified Intermediary (QI). The QI serves as a mandatory, independent third party, crucial for navigating the complex rules and safeguarding exchange funds to comply with IRS safe harbor regulations.

This report has provided a directory of identified U.S.-based 1031 QIs, compiled from extensive research. The market landscape is characterized by fragmentation, with numerous providers, but also a significant trend towards consolidation. Many of the largest and most prominent QIs are subsidiaries of major title insurance companies or financial institutions, leveraging parent company strength to offer enhanced security and nationwide reach.

Crucially, there is limited federal regulation specific to 1031 QIs beyond independence requirements. This underscores the absolute necessity for investors and their advisors to conduct rigorous due diligence when selecting a provider. Key evaluation criteria include the QI's experience, specific expertise, robust procedures for ensuring the security of exchange funds (including segregated accounts, adequate insurance/bonding, and internal controls), customer service responsiveness, and fee transparency. The Certified Exchange Specialist® (CES®) designation serves as a valuable indicator of specialized expertise.

Potential confusion exists regarding the term "Qualified Intermediary," as the IRS maintains a separate "QI list" related to international tax withholding, which is entirely distinct from Section 1031 accommodators.

### **B. Final Recommendations for Users**

Based on the analysis presented, the following recommendations are offered to those seeking to engage a Section 1031 Qualified Intermediary:

1. **Utilize Multiple Resources for Identification:** Begin the search using the directory provided in Section III.B of this report, but supplement this by consulting the Federation of Exchange Accommodators (FEA) online member directory 8 and seeking referrals from trusted professional advisors (experienced CPAs, real estate attorneys, title officers).12  
2. **Prioritize Due Diligence:** Do not select a QI based solely on name recognition or fee structure. Employ the detailed checklist provided in Section V.A to thoroughly vet potential candidates.  
3. **Focus Intensely on Fund Security:** Pay paramount attention to the QI's policies and procedures for safeguarding exchange funds. Verify the use of segregated accounts, inquire about FDIC insurance strategies for large balances, confirm substantial fidelity bond and E\&O insurance coverage limits, and investigate the availability of parent company guarantees if applicable.6 Remember that the security of funds is non-negotiable.  
4. **Verify Credentials and Experience:** Confirm the specific experience of the firm and the assigned personnel with transactions similar to the one contemplated. Look for relevant credentials like the CES® designation.6  
5. **Consult Professional Advisors:** Always discuss the selection of a QI and the structure of the exchange with qualified legal and tax advisors before entering into any agreements.12 The QI facilitates the exchange but typically does not provide tax or legal advice.13  
6. **Avoid the IRS QI List Confusion:** Be aware that the official IRS "QI list" 27 pertains to withholding agents for international payments and is  
   **not** a directory of Section 1031 exchange accommodators. Do not use it for this purpose.

By following a methodical identification process and conducting comprehensive due diligence with a strong focus on fund security and provider expertise, exchangers can significantly increase the likelihood of a smooth, compliant, and successful Section 1031 tax-deferred exchange.
